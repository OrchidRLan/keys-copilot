    Keys Copilot

    An AI arranging partner for a physical MIDI keyboard. I play a melody and ask for chords by voice; the AI writes them into Ableton Live, and while it plays, the chord notes it added light up on my own keys in mixed reality.

    Author Ruolan Chen (rc975) Package xrc_students.keys_copilot.chen.rc975 Built on XRC Core · XRC AI 0.6.0 · XRC Speech 0.1.1 Hardware Meta Quest · Roland GO:KEYS 5 · Windows PC with Ableton Live 12 Suite

    Summary

    When you write music in a DAW, you keep switching between the instrument, the mouse and the screen. Keys Copilot lets you stay at the keyboard. The experience has two parts:

    Part A · Voice and agents

    Ask for chords

    With a single-note melody in Ableton, I say "Add some chords to my melody." The Studio Assistant agent reads the melody through a tool call, asks the Arranger agent (used as a tool) for a progression, writes it to the Chords track, and plays everything from bar 1.

    Part B · Mixed reality

    See the added notes

    While it plays, the notes of the sounding chord light up on my physical keys in passthrough, with note names, so I can see exactly which notes the AI added under my melody. The next chord is previewed one beat early.

    My hands are on the keys and my eyes are on the keys. Voice and AI replace the mouse. Mixed reality puts the information onto the keys.

    Why AI, why XR

    I checked each part by asking what the project loses if that part is removed.

    Without…What breaks
    AIVague intent ("lo-fi", "funkier", "slower") no longer becomes concrete musical operations. I would have to know the theory and enter chords by hand. The agent reads the context (my melody and its key) and decides what to do.
    XRThe added notes would only exist on a screen I have to look up at while playing. Only mixed reality can show "these are the notes under your melody" directly on the physical keys. PianoVision has already shown that on-key guidance works for learning.
    VoiceBoth hands are busy on the keys, so the only remaining input would be a mouse or a controller, and both take my hands off the instrument.

    Demo script

    1. Put on the headset (connected to the PC by USB). Passthrough shows the room and the GO:KEYS 5.
    2. Calibrate the level keyboard: align the cyan crosshair with the LEFT FRONT OUTER white-key corner, hold still for 0.3 seconds and press left X; repeat at the RIGHT FRONT OUTER corner. Key spacing fits the measured span. Follow four MIDI checks, then press X only if the overlay visually aligns; left Y restarts. Saved positions require review each session.
    3. The melody: Twinkle Twinkle, 4 bars, recorded in Ableton's Melody track. Alternatively, play it freely and say "Play back what I just played"; the AI saves the phrase with its tempo detected and plays it back.
    4. Part A. Hold A on the right controller and say "Add some chords to my melody." The status strip and trace show: listening → thinking → Arranger is writing chords → writing the arrangement to Ableton.
    5. Part B. Ableton plays melody and chords from bar 1. On the real keys, the sounding chord lights up with note names (root in orange), the next chord glows faintly one beat before it starts, and the label above the keys reads, for example, C → F. The assistant says what it played and tells me to watch the keys.
    6. Say "Make it an arpeggio" or "Make it funky." The pattern changes and the lights follow the new clip.
    7. Say "How do I play the first chord?" The chord stays lit for practice. Keys I play correctly turn green; wrong keys flash red.

    Why there is a PC bridge

    This project uses a PC MIDI relay so MIDI input and Ableton control share one bridge. Direct USB/BLE MIDI on Quest is outside the tested deployment path; no universal platform-support claim is required for this design. The bridge is a small Python script that ships with the package. It streams key presses to the headset, pushes Ableton's playhead, and forwards a fixed whitelist of commands to Ableton.

    Wired link between headset and PC

    The headset connects to the PC with a USB-C cable, and the bridge runs adb reverse tcp:9000 tcp:9000 whenever a headset is plugged in, so the app reaches the bridge at 127.0.0.1:9000. This avoids two networks I can't control: my apartment's managed WhiteSky Wi-Fi and campus eduroam, which both block device-to-device traffic. The player sits at the keyboard anyway, so the cable costs nothing. adb reverse only carries TCP, so the headset and bridge exchange newline-delimited JSON over one connection, which also means no command is lost. Wi-Fi is used only to reach the OpenAI API. During development I run the scene from the Unity Editor over Quest Link.

    Ableton control

    • ableton-mcp Remote Script (MIT), installed in Live's MIDI Remote Scripts folder: writes and reads clips, controls transport, tempo and the playhead. Its socket has no authentication, so it stays on loopback and only the bridge talks to it.
    • The bridge adds three helpers: write_clip (replace a clip in one step), find_track (tracks are found by name; on my set the Chords track turned out to be index 5, not 1) and play_from_start (stop, move the playhead to beat 0, launch the clips together).
    • File import and other risky commands are not on the whitelist.

    Part A: agents and tools

    Key design decision: the LLM chooses chords; code places the notes

    General-purpose LLMs are weak at rhythm and bar alignment (see the MusicTheoryBench and ChatMusician results). So the Arranger never outputs MIDI. It returns chord symbols and a pattern name. Deterministic C# code (ChordVoicer, PatternLibrary) turns them into pitches and timings, with smooth voice leading. Every note is on Ableton's grid, and the same plan always produces the same notes. Invalid plans return actionable validation errors. The agent may correct validation errors, but must not blindly retry a write with an unknown or partial outcome.

    { "key": "C major", "bpm": 85, "bars": 4, "pattern": "lofi",
  "chords": [ {"bar":1,"symbol":"C"},
              {"bar":2,"symbol":"F"},  {"bar":2,"beat":3,"symbol":"C"},
              {"bar":3,"symbol":"F"},  {"bar":3,"beat":3,"symbol":"C"},
              {"bar":4,"symbol":"G7"}, {"bar":4,"beat":3,"symbol":"C"} ] }

    Patterns: block, pad, lofi, broken, arpeggio, stabs.

    Two ways to get the melody

    • Recorded in Ableton against the metronome: read back with get_clip_notes, already in beats.
    • Played freely, no metronome: CaptureMelody takes the last phrase from the live key stream and infers the tempo. A rough tempo from the median gap between notes is refined over all notes at once (search within ±15%, then least squares), so timing errors don't accumulate; notes snap to an eighth-note grid. A human-timed Twinkle comes out at 86 BPM with every note on its beat. It never overwrites a clip I recorded in Ableton without asking.
    ToolProviderPurpose
    GetRecordedMelody()AbletonToolsThe melody bar by bar, with tempo, meter and an estimated key (Krumhansl–Schmuckler)
    CaptureMelody(useAbletonTempo, replaceRecording)AbletonToolsSaves what I just played as the Melody clip, tempo detected
    WriteArrangement(planJson)AbletonToolsValidates and voices the plan, replaces the Chords clip, plays from bar 1
    PlayMelody(), Play(), Stop(), SetTempo(bpm)AbletonToolsMelody alone, melody with chords from bar 1, stop, tempo
    HighlightKeys(what, includeBass), ClearHighlights()KeyboardToolsPractice highlights for a chord symbol, notes, or "bar N" of the last arrangement
    GetLastPlayedNotes(seconds)KeyboardToolsDescribes live playing and names chords ("what chord is this?")
    StartCalibration()KeyboardToolsStarts overlay calibration by voice
    ArrangerAgent as toolMelody + key + style → chord plan JSON

    Tool calls arrive on background threads, so anything that touches the scene goes through MainThreadUtility.Invoke.

    Part B: how the lights stay in time

    1. Every playback goes through play_from_start: the bridge stops Live, moves the playhead to beat 0, and launches the melody and chord clips together. The position in the arrangement is then the song beat modulo the arrangement length.
    2. ableton-mcp has no playhead listener, so the bridge reads the playhead about 20 times a second while playing and pushes {playing, beat, tempo} to the headset.
    3. BridgeClient advances the beat between updates using the tempo, so the lights don't step.
    4. ArrangementPlan.Voice() gives each chord's span and the exact pitches written to Live, including the bass. ArrangementFollower lights those keys, so what lights up is what sounds. A unit test checks that lit keys equal written notes for every chord.
    On the keysMeaning
    Orange, bold note nameRoot of the chord that is sounding
    Amber, note nameOther notes the AI added, including the left-hand bass
    Faint amberThe next chord, one beat before it starts
    BlueA key I'm pressing (my melody)
    Green / redPractice mode only: a highlighted key I hit / a key outside the chord. During playback my melody notes are never marked wrong.

    Above the keys, a label shows the current and next chord (C → F).

    Feedback layer

    XRC AI's AgentEvents reports agent runs but not tool calls, so my tools raise their own toolInvoked, toolCompleted and toolFailed events. Feedback components subscribe through UnityEvents; XRC AI and XRC Speech are not modified.

    GO:KEYS alignment — implementation review, 6 October 2026

    Decision: use MIDI for note identity, measured manual calibration for keyboard placement, and a procedural key overlay for feedback. No camera recognition or GO:KEYS body model is required for this MVP. MIDI checks verify the configured pitch mapping; only the user can confirm spatial alignment in this implementation.

    Implemented nowBehavior and boundary
    Two explicit outer cornersSample the top front-left OUTER corner of the first white key and the top front-right OUTER corner of the last, with keys unpressed. Do not sample the case or key centers.
    Measured horizontal spacingWhite-key pitch = horizontal outer-edge span / white-key count: span / 36 for the default 61-key C-to-C layout. Center-to-center endpoints would require /35 and a half-key offset; that endpoint convention is not implemented.
    Stable capture and thin crosshairAt least six recent samples spanning 0.18 s within a 0.30 s window, with each sample within 3 mm of their mean. Moving or insufficient samples request a retry. This reduces jitter, not systematic tracking or pointer-offset error.
    Level-keyboard guardReject endpoint height differences over 8 mm and fitted spacing outside 18–30 mm. Both ends must be white keys; at least one octave is required. The 61-key default is MIDI 36–96 and is checked against actual input.
    Four MIDI checks + visual acceptanceDefault sequence: leftmost white C2 (36), middle white F4 (65), rightmost white C7 (96), leftmost black C#2 (37). Cyan marks the requested key; held keys are blue. Wrong pitches do not advance. After all four, X confirms visual alignment and saves; Y restarts.
    Session reviewStore pose, measured key pitch and MIDI range in a versioned local profile. Old pose-only data is ignored. On restart, show the saved outline for review: X begins the four checks; Y recalibrates. A saved pose is not a spatial anchor.

    Limits: two points do not determine a full plane. This version assumes a level keyboard and does not detect front-to-back tilt. White/black key depths and black-key offsets remain approximate; width fitting alone cannot correct them. A moved keyboard or changed tracking origin requires recalibration. No automatic tracking-loss/recenter recovery, fine-adjustment UI, third-point mode, persistent spatial anchor or camera localization is implemented.

    Next stages: first measure on-device alignment at left, middle and right, including black keys; then add a third non-collinear point for tilted keyboards and a provider-supported persistent anchor with localization failure fallback. An anchor fixed in the room does not follow a moved keyboard. If automatic localization becomes necessary, evaluate a fixed AprilTag with a measured tag-to-keybed transform. Quest 3/3S camera access is available with the documented permissions; choosing manual calibration is a scope decision.

    How to test this build

    1. Editor regression: open the implementation project; Window → General → Test Runner → EditMode. Run the xrc_students.keys_copilot.chen.rc975.Tests assembly. Include KeyboardCalibrationLogicTests, CalibrationPointSamplerTests and KeyboardCalibrationVerificationTests.
    2. Input: connect GO:KEYS by USB, start Bridge~/.venv/Scripts/python keys_bridge.py, and check the MIDI input log. Ableton reachability is needed for arranging but not for the four spatial-calibration MIDI checks. Stop Ableton playback while checking alignment.
    3. Quest: build/run the Keys Copilot scene with passthrough. Existing scenes gain calibration feedback automatically; no scene regeneration is needed. Verify KeyboardCalibration references the overlay and tracked right controller, Camera has the MainCamera tag, and the overlay hierarchy has unit scale.
    4. Calibrate: keep the keyboard level. Align the right-controller crosshair with the left outer corner, hold still 0.3 seconds, press left X; repeat on the right outer corner. Follow MIDI 36 → 65 → 96 → 37, release each key, inspect white and black alignment, then X to confirm. Press Y at any point to restart. Right A/B remain start/stop speech.
    5. Negative tests: press X while moving the pointer (retry); sample the same corner twice (reject); use different corner heights over 8 mm (reject); press a wrong or octave-shifted MIDI pitch (stay on the same check). Recalibrating must leave status and chord-label objects intact.
    6. Restart: close/reopen the app. The saved pose must be shown as a preview requiring review, not automatically trusted. Move the keyboard or recenter, then press Y and repeat calibration. To check rejection without overwriting a good profile, exit before final confirmation.
    7. End-to-end: each Ableton MIDI track needs an instrument, and Melody slot 1 needs the recorded 4-bar melody. Request simple chords, then practice the first chord. Verify audio, written notes, MR lights and return from practice to playback. Hearing only the metronome is an audio/clip-routing issue, separate from spatial calibration.

    Validation record: Unity 6000.4.11f1 EditMode: 87 passed, 0 failed (6 October 2026); PC bridge fake-Live regressions: 14 passed. The package Runtime, Editor and Tests assemblies compile. Physical Quest alignment, audio/visual timing and Android IL2CPP acceptance are not yet measured. HTML content and structure checked statically; browser rendering preview was unavailable because local file URLs are blocked by the browser tool.

    Device acceptance target: record repeatability across three calibrations and inspect left/middle/right plus black keys. A suggested target is no adjacent-key overlap and estimated center error below 5 mm at the checked positions. This is an acceptance target, not a measured accuracy claim. Record calibration time and remaining front/back or black-key errors.
